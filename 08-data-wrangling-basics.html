8  Data wrangling: basics

8.1 Overview

Section 1 ended with you loading a real data file into R with read.csv(). The result was a data frame, and you saw how to inspect it (head, str, summary, nrow, ncol). What you have not yet done is manipulate the data: pull out specific columns, filter rows that meet a condition, create derived variables, or compute summaries for subgroups.

This chapter covers those operations. Together they are usually called data wrangling: the work of turning a raw data file into the specific subsets and summaries you need to actually answer a research question. Good data wrangling is the foundation for everything that follows. Most of the time you spend on a real analysis is spent here, not on the statistical model itself.

We will work with one dataset throughout the chapter, and continue to use it in the chapters that follow.

8.2 The example dataset

The dataset is called vgames_study.csv. It comes from a (fictional) study on the relationship between video-game playing habits and self-reported aggression. It has 120 participants and eight variables.

Variable Type Meaning
participant_id integer Unique participant identifier (1 to 120)
age integer Participant age in years
gender character Self-reported gender (man, woman, non-binary)
condition character Experimental condition (violent or neutral video game)
hours_weekly numeric Self-reported hours per week spent playing video games
trait_hostility numeric Score on a hostility trait scale, 1 to 7
empathy numeric Score on an empathy scale, 1 to 7
aggression numeric Self-reported aggression after the experimental session, 1 to 10

The design is a between-participants experiment: 60 participants were randomly assigned to play a violent video game and 60 to play a neutral video game. After the session, all 120 reported their aggression. The other variables (age, gender, hours of weekly play, trait hostility, empathy) were measured before the experiment.

This dataset is small and clean, which keeps the examples readable. The wrangling operations you will learn on it are exactly the same as on a real dataset of any size.

8.2.1 Getting the dataset

Download the file from https://y45hv1n.github.io/intro-data-analysis/data/vgames_study.csv and place it inside a folder called data in your R project. The path you will read from will then be "data/vgames_study.csv".

If you have not yet created an R project for this book, do that first (review Chapter 3 if you need to). For everything that follows, we assume you are inside an R project and that the file lives at data/vgames_study.csv.

8.3 Loading the data

Inside your R project, in a new script, load the data and take a quick look:

data <- read.csv("data/vgames_study.csv")

head(data)
str(data)

head(data) prints the first six rows. str(data) prints a structural summary: 120 observations, 8 variables, the type of each variable. You should see participant_id, age, and hours_weekly as numeric, and gender and condition as character (text).

The object data is now in your environment and you can refer to it by name.

A note on the object name. Calling the object data is convenient and harmless in this chapter, but data is also the name of a base R function (?data). For larger projects, a more descriptive name is better, for example vgames or study. We use data here because it matches the homework conventions you may already have seen.

8.4 Accessing one variable: the dollar sign $

Chapter 5 introduced the dollar sign $ as the operator that pulls one component out of a composite object. On a data frame, $ extracts a single column. The general form is dataframe$column_name.

data$aggression
data$age
data$condition

Each of these prints an entire column as a vector. data$aggression is a numeric vector of 120 aggression scores; data$condition is a character vector of 120 condition labels.

Once you have a vector, you can do anything to it that you would do to any other vector. For example, the average aggression score across all 120 participants:

mean(data$aggression)

The expression is read inside-out: data$aggression produces a vector of 120 numbers, and mean() reduces that vector to a single number. The same inside-out logic applies whenever a function is called on a column.

8.5 Creating new variables

You can add a new column to a data frame by assigning into a name that does not yet exist on the right of $:

data$aggression_doubled <- data$aggression * 2

After this line, data has nine variables instead of eight. The new column aggression_doubled contains every aggression score multiplied by two. Look at the Environment panel: the description of data should now read 120 obs. of 9 variables.

A more useful example. Compute each participant’s deviation from the mean aggression score. The mean is a single number; subtracting it from each value gives, for each participant, how far above or below the average they scored.

data$aggression_deviation <- data$aggression - mean(data$aggression)

R applies the subtraction element-wise: for every row, it computes (this participant’s aggression) minus (the overall mean aggression). The result is a new column with 120 values, some positive (above the mean), some negative (below).

To inspect the new column alongside the original, select both columns:

head(data[, c("aggression", "aggression_deviation")])

This previews the next topic: subsetting columns by name. We come back to it shortly.

The general pattern is dataframe$new_column <- expression. The expression on the right can use other columns of the same data frame, scalar values, or any R operation that produces a vector of the right length. New variables defined this way persist for the rest of the session and become part of data.

8.6 Subsetting rows: filtering by a logical condition

You will frequently want to look at only a subset of your participants: those in the violent condition, those over 25, those with high trait hostility. The general tool for this is logical subsetting.

Two-dimensional bracket indexing on a data frame uses the form dataframe[rows, columns], where leaving a slot empty means “all.” Chapter 5 introduced this with positional indices (data[1, ] for the first row, data[, 2] for the second column). For real research, you will more often select rows by a condition.

The condition uses the double-equals operator ==, which means “is equal to.” (Single = is not a comparison in R; it is used inside function calls for named arguments. Confusing the two is a common early mistake.) For each row, R checks whether the condition is TRUE or FALSE, and the subsetting keeps only the rows where it is TRUE.

To select only participants in the violent condition:

violent_group <- data[data$condition == "violent", ]
nrow(violent_group)
# returns 60

Read the bracket from left to right. The expression data$condition == "violent" returns a logical vector of length 120 (TRUE for the 60 violent-condition participants, FALSE for the 60 neutral-condition participants). The bracket then keeps only the rows where the logical vector is TRUE. The empty slot after the comma means “all columns.”

The result violent_group is a smaller data frame with 60 rows and the same 8 (or 9, if you added new variables) columns.

Other comparisons work the same way. Some you will use often:

data[data$age > 25, ]                  # all rows where age is greater than 25
data[data$age >= 25, ]                 # age greater than or equal to 25
data[data$gender == "non-binary", ]    # only non-binary participants
data[data$gender != "man", ]           # everyone except those who reported "man"
data[data$hours_weekly < 5, ]          # participants who play fewer than 5 hours weekly

The operators are the standard ones: == (equal to), != (not equal to), <, <=, >, >=. The result of any of these comparisons is a logical vector of TRUE and FALSE values, the same length as the column being compared.

8.7 Subsetting columns: by name

You can also select specific columns. The general form, again, is dataframe[rows, columns]. To keep all rows but only some columns, leave the row slot empty and pass a character vector of column names in the column slot:

data_subset <- data[, c("aggression", "condition")]
head(data_subset)

c("aggression", "condition") is a character vector with two names, so the bracket returns a data frame with all 120 rows and just those two columns, in that order.

For a single column, you could write either:

data[, "aggression"]    # bracket form, returns a vector
data$aggression         # $ form, returns the same vector

Both work. The $ form is shorter and is what you will use for one-off column access. The bracket form generalizes to multiple columns, which is why it is the right tool when you want a sub-data-frame.

8.8 Combining: filter rows and select columns at once

Both slots of the bracket can be filled at the same time. To get only the aggression scores of the violent-condition participants:

violent_aggression <- data[data$condition == "violent", "aggression"]
violent_aggression

The first slot filters rows (only violent condition); the second slot selects one column (aggression). Because we asked for a single column, R returns a vector, not a data frame. violent_aggression is a numeric vector of 60 aggression scores.

This single-line idiom is one of the most useful patterns in base R for inspecting subgroups. Once you know it, many small questions about your data become one-liners.

8.9 Computing summaries by group

Putting the previous two pieces together, you can now compute summary statistics separately for each subgroup of interest. The first practical question of any experiment with two conditions: do the two conditions differ on the outcome?

mean(data[data$condition == "violent", "aggression"])
# returns 5.558333

mean(data[data$condition == "neutral", "aggression"])
# returns 4.213333

Two lines. Two numbers. The participants who played the violent game reported, on average, an aggression score of about 5.56; those who played the neutral game reported about 4.21. The violent-condition mean is higher by roughly 1.35 points on the 1-to-10 scale.

You can do the same with any other summary function (sd, median, min, max) and any other grouping variable. For example, the mean weekly playing hours separately by gender:

mean(data[data$gender == "man", "hours_weekly"])
mean(data[data$gender == "woman", "hours_weekly"])
mean(data[data$gender == "non-binary", "hours_weekly"])

If your dataset has many groups, this approach becomes repetitive and you will eventually want a function that does it for you in one line (we will introduce one in a later chapter). For two or three groups, the pattern above is short, transparent, and entirely sufficient.

8.10 Looking ahead: the mean as a model

Let us pause on what you just did. With two means, you summarized the responses of 120 people. Each of those participants is unique: a particular age, a particular hostility score, a particular history with video games. Those individual differences disappear into a single number. The mean tells you the typical aggression score for each condition, but it tells you nothing about the spread of scores within each condition, nothing about the shape of the distribution, and nothing about how individual participants varied.

That tradeoff is general. Whenever you summarize a group of people with a single number, you compress information; whatever that number does not capture is lost (at least temporarily). The art of statistical analysis is choosing summaries that capture what you actually want to know, and being explicit about what they leave out.

In the chapters that follow, we will treat the mean not as a number but as the simplest possible model of a dataset: a guess about each individual that is the same for everyone. Real models will use information about each participant (their condition, their age, their hostility) to make better, more individualized guesses. The starting point is always the mean, and the question is always: how much better can we do than the mean?
