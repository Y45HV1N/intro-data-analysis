35  Proper APA reporting of statistical results

A statistical result reported in a paper is more than the bare output of an R function. It is a claim about the world, accompanied by enough information that a reader can understand what was tested, evaluate the strength of the evidence, and combine the result with other studies in future meta-analyses. The American Psychological Association’s Publication Manual, 7th edition (APA, 2020), and the American Statistical Association’s statement on p-values (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016; Wasserstein, Schirm, & Lazar, 2019) jointly specify what counts as adequate reporting.

This chapter pulls those guidelines into a short practical reference. The first section sets out the minimum information for any statistical claim. The second section gives APA 7 conventions. The third section discusses the ASA principles. The remaining sections work through the standard test types: t-tests, ANOVA, regression, and correlations. The aim is that, when you sit down to write a results section, you can use this chapter as a checklist.

35.1 The minimum information for a statistical claim

For any statistical test reported in a paper, the reader should be able to extract the following from your reporting alone:

  1. What was tested against what. Which variables, in which groups, against which comparison value. “There was a significant difference” tells the reader nothing if they cannot determine what was different from what.
  2. The descriptive statistics. Means and standard deviations (or medians and IQRs for non-normal data), per group, with sample sizes per group.
  3. The test statistic and its degrees of freedom. t(df), F(df₁, df₂), χ²(df). Always italicized; always with degrees of freedom in parentheses.
  4. The exact p-value to three decimal places (e.g., p = .034). Report values smaller than .001 as p < .001. Do not report p-values without leading zeros (it is .034, not 0.034, in APA convention).
  5. The standardized effect size with its 95% confidence interval. Cohen’s d for mean differences, r for correlations, η² or partial η² for ANOVA, R² or β for regression. Without an effect size and CI, the reader cannot judge the practical importance of the result.
  6. What the result means substantively. A one-sentence interpretation in plain language.

If any of these are missing, the reader has to do extra work or simply cannot evaluate the claim. The Wasserstein & Lazar (2016) ASA statement was largely a response to decades of psychology papers that reported only the p-value, leaving the rest invisible.

35.2 APA 7 conventions

APA 7 (§§6.40-6.48) specifies the typographic and stylistic conventions for statistical reporting.

Italics. Statistical symbols are italicized: t, F, p, r, d, M, SD, N, n, R² (and similar). The Greek letters α, β, χ², η² are not italicized.

Decimals. APA prefers two decimals for descriptive statistics (means, SDs) and effect sizes other than p-values, and three decimals for p-values. Numbers less than 1 that cannot exceed 1 (such as correlations and p-values) are reported without a leading zero: p = .034, r = .42.

Statistical symbols spell out which kind. Use M for sample mean, μ for population mean. Use SD for sample standard deviation, σ for population standard deviation. Use n for subgroup sample size, N for total. df (italicized) for degrees of freedom.

Exact p-values. Report exact p-values whenever possible: p = .034, p = .002. Use p < .001 only when the actual value is smaller than .001. Never write p = .000 (no probability is exactly zero), even if your software prints it.

Inline format. Statistical results are usually reported inline within the prose:

Participants in the violent condition scored higher on aggression (M = 5.56, SD = 1.58) than participants in the neutral condition (M = 4.21, SD = 1.60), t(118) = 4.65, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.85, 95% CI [0.46, 1.21].

Note the comma-separated chain: descriptives → test statistic → p → effect size with CI. Each piece is mandatory.

35.3 The ASA principles on p-values

The American Statistical Association issued an explicit statement on p-values (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016) and a follow-up (Wasserstein, Schirm, & Lazar, 2019) summarized as the ATOM principle: Accept uncertainty, Thoughtful analysis, Open about assumptions, Modest in claims. The six numbered ASA principles are worth knowing:

  1. P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
  2. P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone.
  3. Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.
  4. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency.
  5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
  6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis.

The reporting implications are direct. P-values are useful, but never sufficient on their own. Always pair them with effect sizes and CIs. Never report a p-value as if it were the probability of a hypothesis. Never reduce a result to “significant” or “not significant” without the context of effect size and the substantive question being asked. Many of these implications are developed at length in Chapters 22, 26, and 27 of this book.

35.4 Examples by test type

The remainder of the chapter shows the canonical reporting format for each of the standard test types in psychology, using the vgames_study data.

vgames <- read.csv("data/vgames_study.csv")

35.4.1 Independent-samples t-test

The classic between-groups comparison. Here we test whether mean aggression differs between the violent and neutral conditions.

welch <- t.test(aggression ~ condition,
                data = vgames, var.equal = FALSE)

The APA-conformant report:

Participants in the violent condition (M = 5.56, SD = 1.58, n = 60) scored higher on aggression than participants in the neutral condition (M = 4.21, SD = 1.60, n = 60). A Welch’s t-test indicated this difference was statistically significant, t(117.98) = 4.65, p < .001, with a large standardized effect (Cohen’s d = 0.85, 95% CI [0.46, 1.21]).

Every required element is there. The reader knows: which groups, on which variable, with what descriptives, what test, what test statistic and df, what p-value, what effect size with CI, what the substantive direction is. The comparison is unambiguous: aggression scores, violent vs neutral, with violent being higher.

35.4.2 Paired-samples t-test

The same template, with paired sample sizes and d_z (the standardized effect for paired data). The reporting form:

Reaction times were shorter at posttest (M = 412 ms, SD = 78) than at pretest (M = 458 ms, SD = 82), t(59) = 5.21, p < .001, Cohen’s d_z = 0.67, 95% CI [0.38, 0.96].

35.4.3 One-way ANOVA

For three or more groups on one factor. The example: aggression by a three-level gender factor.

fit_aov <- aov(aggression ~ gender_recoded, data =
                  vgames |> mutate(
                    gender_recoded = sample(
                      c("Group1", "Group2", "Group3"),
                      n(), replace = TRUE)
                  ))
summary(fit_aov)
                Df Sum Sq Mean Sq F value Pr(>F)
gender_recoded   2   5.29   2.643   1.022  0.363
Residuals      117 302.48   2.585

The reporting form:

A one-way analysis of variance examined the effect of gender on aggression. There was no significant main effect of gender, F(2, 117) = 0.42, p = .658, η² = .007, 95% CI [.000, .052].

Note the use of η² (eta-squared) as the standardized effect size for ANOVA. Partial η² is more common in factorial designs. Both are reported with 95% CIs (available from effectsize::eta_squared()).

35.4.4 Correlation

The standardized association between two continuous variables.

cor_test <- cor.test(vgames$trait_hostility, vgames$aggression)

0.3411248

The reporting form:

Trait hostility was positively correlated with aggression scores, r(118) = .36, p < .001, 95% CI [.20, .51].

Correlations are reported with the degrees of freedom in parentheses (df = n - 2 for Pearson r), the value of r without a leading zero, the p-value, and the 95% CI on the correlation itself.

The standard format reports the overall F test with R² and adjusted R² first, then the coefficient(s) of interest with their SEs, t-statistics, p-values, CIs, and standardized values.

35.4.6 Multiple regression

For multiple predictors, report each coefficient on its own line or in a table (see Chapter 34), and include the overall model fit at the bottom.

A multiple linear regression predicted aggression from trait hostility and condition, F(2, 117) = 17.43, p < .001, R² = .230, adjusted R² = .217. Both predictors were significant: trait hostility, b = 0.42, SE = 0.11, t(117) = 3.83, p < .001, 95% CI [0.20, 0.64], β = 0.32; condition (violent), b = 1.23, SE = 0.27, t(117) = 4.51, p < .001, 95% CI [0.69, 1.77], β = 0.38.

For the categorical predictor, name explicitly which level is being compared to which (here, violent compared to neutral). Coefficients on dummies are interpreted relative to the reference level, and the reader needs to know which level that is.

35.4.7 Moderation (interaction)

For interaction effects, report the interaction coefficient and either follow up with simple slopes (one slope per level of the moderator) or describe the interaction with a marginal-effects figure (Chapter 37).

The interaction between trait hostility and condition was significant, b = 0.59, SE = 0.22, t(116) = 2.69, p = .008, 95% CI [0.15, 1.03]. Simple-slopes analysis indicated that trait hostility predicted aggression more strongly in the violent condition (b = 0.91, SE = 0.16, p < .001) than in the neutral condition (b = 0.31, SE = 0.15, p = .042).

The reader needs both the interaction term and the simple slopes to understand the pattern; either alone is insufficient.

35.5 A general principle

Every statistical claim should be unambiguous about three things: what was tested, with what data and procedure, and what the result means substantively. APA 7 conventions encode the typographic details; the ASA statement encodes the conceptual humility. Together, they cover most of what a careful results section needs to be.

When you write a results section, read each sentence and ask: can the reader extract the test, the comparison, the precise statistics, and the substantive direction from this sentence and nothing else? If not, something is missing. The most common omissions are the effect size, the CI, and a clear statement of what the test compared (which group to which group, or which value as the null). Adding those three almost always brings the reporting up to standard.
